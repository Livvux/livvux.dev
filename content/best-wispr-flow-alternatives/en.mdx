**My #1 Wispr Flow alternative is [FluidVoice](https://fluidvoice.com/). My #2 is [VoiceInk](https://tryvoiceink.com/).** Both make a compelling case that good AI dictation on a Mac does not need an ongoing subscription.

I like the idea behind Wispr Flow: press a shortcut, speak naturally, and get text that reads as though you had typed it. What I do not like is paying **$15 per month, or $144 per year on an annual Pro plan**, when much of what I actually need can run directly on my Mac. To be fair, [Wispr Flow has a free plan](https://wisprflow.ai/pricing), and its paid version offers real advantages. But for everyday dictation, developer prompts and local AI text cleanup, I would start elsewhere.

**My ranking is about value and workflow fit, not a claim that FluidVoice has won a controlled speed or accuracy benchmark.** As of **October 15, 2026**, the evidence supports a preference, not a universal winner.

## #1 — FluidVoice: the one I would install first

**[FluidVoice](https://fluidvoice.com/) is my personal pick because it combines quick feedback while speaking, on-device transcription, local AI text cleanup and free access.** I am not looking for another SaaS subscription just to get a well-formatted prompt into a terminal or editor.

### 1. Live dictation: you can see the text forming

FluidVoice has a real-time transcription preview rather than making you speak blindly and wait for a complete paragraph to appear. That matters in longer prompts: I can notice whether the recognizer followed my sentence and correct my phrasing before I lose the thought.

FluidVoice supports different speech engines, including **Fluid Speech Mini, Parakeet variants, Nemotron Speech, Whisper and Apple Speech**. The app's [official model lineup](https://fluidvoice.com/) is worth checking because support for German, English and other languages varies by model. In particular, some Parakeet variants are English-only, while others support multilingual use.

This is an advantage in **experience**, not yet an established win on latency: [VoiceInk also supports real-time processing and partial text](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/mode-settings). Anyone asserting that one is objectively faster needs the same microphone, hardware, language, audio samples and measurement procedure.

### 2. Fluid Intelligence: local AI beyond raw transcription

A recognizer answers: *What sounds were spoken?* A post-processing model answers: *What should the final text look like?*

FluidVoice uses its **Fluid-1 family** to remove filler words, recover spoken corrections, improve capitalization and punctuation, and format the result. Its current Mac lineup includes **Fluid-1, Fluid-1 Mini and Fluid-1 Pico**; the smaller options are intended to reduce the download and hardware burden. The processing happens locally in a default on-device configuration. The app also offers optional OpenAI, Groq and custom enhancement providers, which naturally change the privacy and cost equation. [Source](https://fluidvoice.com/)

This matters for the kind of prompt I actually dictate. A raw recording might read:

> okay so uh check the next js app and see why the cloudflare deploy failed yesterday, do not change production until tests pass, and give me a short list of the problems

What I want is closer to:

> Investigate yesterday's Cloudflare deployment failure in the Next.js app. Run the relevant tests before modifying production. Report the root cause and a prioritized list of fixes.

That is an **illustrative before/after**, not the measured output of a particular Fluid-1 release. Local rewriting is useful, but always review names, commands, paths and intended meaning. A cleanup model should not silently change what you asked for.

### 3. Write Mode, Command Mode and ordinary dictation

FluidVoice has three especially useful ways of working:

- **Direct Dictation:** insert what I said into the active field.
- **Write Mode:** write or rewrite text directly in another app, including selected text.
- **Command Mode:** issue voice-driven Mac actions such as opening applications or launching supported shortcuts.

For developers, I see the value in moving between an AI chat, a terminal, a code editor and GitHub without treating voice input as a separate document-writing task. [The GitHub README documents these modes](https://github.com/altic-dev/FluidVoice#features), and the website also describes app-aware tone profiles.

A reasonable safety rule: inspect voice-triggered actions before using them for destructive terminal operations, shell scripts or production changes.

### 4. Free means no recurring app bill

FluidVoice's official site advertises **free use without subscription tiers**. Downloading local speech and enhancement models may use several gigabytes, but local inference does not require per-minute transcription payments. Optional hosted APIs, if you enable them, can have their own costs.

On macOS, the app requires **macOS 15 or later**. Apple Silicon is the best target for the accelerated local models; Intel Mac support exists through Whisper. The website lists **Windows and iPhone beta builds**, but feature and model parity with the stable Mac application should not be assumed. [Source](https://fluidvoice.com/)

### The open-source caveat people should know

The [FluidVoice application repository](https://github.com/altic-dev/FluidVoice) is licensed under **GPLv3**, but its maintainers explicitly describe **Fluid Intelligence as a separately maintained, private local AI runtime**. That means “the application is open source” is accurate; “every component and model behind FluidVoice is open source” is not.

Privacy also needs precision. The README says audio, transcripts and prompt content stay local unless you configure a cloud provider. It separately documents **anonymous usage analytics sent in weekly batches**, with detailed analytics enabled by default and a setting to disable that detail. Local dictation does not mean the application never makes any network request. See the [repository's Privacy & Analytics section](https://github.com/altic-dev/FluidVoice#privacy--analytics).

**My verdict on FluidVoice:** I would choose it first for ordinary dictation and cleaned-up technical prompts. Free, local-first and fast-feeling is already a very strong combination.

## #2 — VoiceInk: the better choice when customization matters most

**[VoiceInk](https://tryvoiceink.com/) is the app I would try next if I found myself repeatedly correcting technical terms or switching between different writing styles and applications.** It is also open source, supports local models, and offers more detailed context controls than the simple “dictate into any app” workflow suggests.

### 1. Modes that follow the app, website or spoken trigger

VoiceInk lets you define separate **Modes** with their own transcription model, language, AI enhancement, prompt, output behavior and shortcut. Modes can activate using:

- The active **application**, such as an editor versus an email client.
- A **website or URL**, useful for different browser workflows.
- A **spoken trigger phrase** at the beginning or end of dictation.
- A dedicated **keyboard shortcut**.

Imagine using one mode to keep technical language literal in your terminal, another to polish email, and a third to restructure spoken notes into clean Markdown for an issue report. That specificity is VoiceInk's main advantage. See [Modes](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/modes) and [Mode Triggers](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/mode-triggers).

FluidVoice now supports application-aware tone prompts too. The distinction is not that FluidVoice is unaware of apps; it is the breadth of VoiceInk's documented **URL, word-trigger, shortcut, context and output configuration**.

### 2. Screen context, selected text and clipboard

VoiceInk's **Context Awareness** can provide an AI-enhanced mode with selected text, clipboard contents and text extracted from the active window. Screen text is captured using local OCR; **the recognized text**, not the screenshot image, is placed in the enhancement context.

This opens workflows that pure dictation cannot handle well: dictate “rewrite this more clearly” with a selected paragraph, refer to a visible issue description, or draft a response based on the text in the current window.

But there is an important privacy condition: if your enhancement provider is cloud-based, that **extracted context text can be sent to it**. Not every mode needs screen-reading permissions, and plain dictation does not need to send context. VoiceInk's [context documentation](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/context-awareness) explains the individual switches and permissions.

### 3. The dictionary is genuinely useful for developers

VoiceInk separates **Vocabulary** from **Word Replacements**. Vocabulary helps recognize domain-specific words; replacements directly rewrite known mishearings and can expand shortcuts.

I would start with names I use every day: **Next.js, TypeScript, QBCore, Cloudflare, GitHub, pnpm and shadcn/ui**. If a recognizer consistently renders a project name incorrectly, a deterministic replacement is often more reliable than asking a general-purpose AI model to guess.

VoiceInk can also learn candidate corrections from edits, with provider-dependent privacy implications. Its [Word Replacements guide](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/word-replacements) explains matching order, export and import.

### 4. VoiceInk also has local AI and real-time transcription

This is worth emphasizing because older comparisons sometimes miss it. **VoiceInk is not limited to recording first and transcribing afterward.** Supported models can process audio while you speak and display partial results. Its local model catalog includes **Parakeet, Nemotron, Whisper, Cohere Transcribe, SenseVoice and Apple Speech**. [Source](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/ai-models)

For cleanup, **VoiceInk Refine** runs locally after transcription on an **Apple Silicon Mac with at least 16 GB RAM**. It handles filler, repetitions, punctuation and spoken corrections, but it is a **fixed cleanup task**: Refine does not support custom prompts or selected-text/screen context. For truly custom local contextual enhancement, VoiceInk supports **Ollama** or a configured local command. [Source](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/voiceink-refine)

That distinction matters: **local speech-to-text**, **local cleanup** and **local contextual AI** are three different capabilities. Always confirm the provider selected for each stage.

### 5. Audio files, history and developer integrations

VoiceInk supports a transcription queue for existing **audio and video files**, searchable local history, an assistant-style recorder response and **Custom Command** output that passes the finished transcript to a local shell command. You can append to a Markdown file, populate a task list or connect to your own scripts. [Overview](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/introduction) · [Custom Commands](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/custom-commands)

These are particularly attractive if you want voice to become part of a repeatable development workflow rather than only a replacement for typing.

### What VoiceInk actually costs

VoiceInk's **prebuilt, supported Mac application is not free forever**. At publication, the [official pricing page](https://tryvoiceink.com/pricing) lists:

| VoiceInk Mac license | One-time price |
| --- | ---: |
| Solo — 1 Mac | **$25** |
| Personal — 2 Macs | **$39** |
| Extended — 3 Macs | **$49** |

The seller offers a **7-day trial**, lifetime updates for the purchased Mac license, and a 14-day refund policy. The [GPLv3 source code](https://github.com/beingpax/VoiceInk) is available for developers to build themselves; paying for the maintained binary, update path and support is a different proposition from paying to access source code.

There is also an **iOS app**, but do not assume a Mac license automatically covers it. The pricing page mentions macOS 14.4, whereas newer [installation docs](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/installation) state **macOS 15 or later**. Verify your exact Mac and version before purchasing.

**My verdict on VoiceInk:** if per-app and per-website modes, screen context and deterministic technical vocabulary are central to your work, VoiceInk could reasonably be your #1 instead.

## Where Wispr Flow still wins

I do not think Wispr Flow is a bad product. I think its **recurring price is difficult to justify for my Mac-only, local-first workflow**.

Its strengths are meaningful:

- **Broad platform coverage:** Mac, Windows, iOS and Android, with dictation in over 100 advertised languages.
- **Minimal local setup:** its transcription runs in the cloud instead of requiring you to choose, download and optimize local models.
- **Meetings and teams:** Notetaker, shared dictionary/snippets, collaboration, organization administration and enterprise controls are outside the central appeal of a small local dictation app.
- **A real free tier:** currently **2,000 words per week on desktop** and **1,000 on mobile**, according to [Wispr's comparison table](https://wisprflow.ai/pricing).

The trade-off is not just price. Wispr says that **dictation always uses cloud processing and requires internet connectivity**. Wispr offers encryption, training opt-outs and cloud-storage controls; that is not equivalent to offline processing. Its privacy documentation says the **model-improvement and dictation-cloud-storage switches default to on for Free/Pro unless restricted**. If privacy is important, review both switches separately. [Cloud limitations](https://docs.wisprflow.ai/articles/4048537120-what-to-expect-from-flow-accuracy-and-known-limitations) · [Data controls, updated October 13, 2026](https://docs.wisprflow.ai/articles/9609615338-Private-Cloud-Sync-and-Data-Sharing-preferences-in-Wispr-Flow)

Wispr also documents **SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 certifications**, enterprise controls and a BAA process for eligible HIPAA workflows. That makes it a more serious option for an organization with formal procurement and compliance needs. A local-first alternative is not automatically compliant with your organization's policies. [Wispr Privacy & Security](https://wisprflow.ai/privacy)

## Privacy comparison: “local” is a configuration, not a sticker

| Question | FluidVoice | VoiceInk | Wispr Flow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Can raw speech be transcribed offline? | **Yes**, with local model | **Yes**, with local model | **No** |
| Can cleanup remain on-device? | **Yes**, Fluid Intelligence | **Yes**, Refine or local Ollama | **No**, normal cloud workflow |
| Can a user-enabled feature send text away? | Yes: optional cloud provider | Yes: cloud enhancement, transcription or Auto Learn | Yes: service processes dictation in cloud |
| Does the app have local history/analytics considerations? | Optional local audio history; separate anonymous usage telemetry | Local history, optional saved audio and provider settings | Separate cloud storage, training and device-history controls |
| Can I audit the full implementation stack? | GPLv3 app, **not the private Fluid Intelligence runtime** | GPLv3 app, dependencies and models require separate review | Not open source |

For the most private setup, choose a local recognition model, choose local cleanup or none, inspect history/telemetry settings, and avoid transmitting clipboard or screen context to a hosted model. “100% private” marketing claims are not a substitute for reading what each active provider receives. See [FluidVoice's README](https://github.com/altic-dev/FluidVoice), [VoiceInk's data documentation](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/privacy-and-data) and [Wispr's data controls](https://wisprflow.ai/data-controls).

## A practical test before choosing: German, English and code

I would not trust an unrepeatable demo as a benchmark. I would use the **same Mac, microphone, input levels and speech samples**, test each app with a realistic setup, and separate recognition from enhancement.

1. **German dictation:** two minutes of natural German with punctuation, corrections and a longer sentence.
2. **English technical prompt:** include Next.js, PostgreSQL, GitHub Actions, Cloudflare, R2, TypeScript and an API endpoint.
3. **Mixed German/English:** switch languages inside sentences and include proper nouns.
4. **Long request:** dictate a multi-paragraph issue report with headings, action items and exact file or command names.
5. **Latency and quality:** record time until first visible text and time until final output; compare exact-word errors before cleanup, then note meaning changes after cleanup.

Do several runs per test. Report **hardware, selected speech model, selected enhancement model, language and internet state**. Speech-model accuracy, text-cleanup quality and apparent UI latency are separate metrics.

**I have not run that controlled head-to-head test.** I therefore would not claim that FluidVoice is provably faster or more accurate than VoiceInk or Wispr Flow. My #1 ranking is an editorial choice grounded in capabilities, price and architecture.

### My recommended setup

I would start by installing FluidVoice from its [official website](https://fluidvoice.com/) or with **Homebrew: brew install --cask fluidvoice**. Download an appropriate **local multilingual speech model** and a compatible **Fluid-1** option, keep cloud enhancement disabled, and dictate into the actual applications you use.

Use it for a week. Count corrections, latency issues and cases where the cleanup changes technical meaning. If app-specific behavior or recurring technical mishearings become a bottleneck, test [VoiceInk's trial](https://tryvoiceink.com/pricing), add vocabulary rules and create separate development/email modes.

This is not about rejecting every subscription on principle. It is about refusing to pay every month for work your own computer can already do.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is FluidVoice really free?

**Yes**, the vendor currently advertises free use with no subscription tiers. Local processing has no recurring app fee. Optional external API providers may charge separately. [Source](https://fluidvoice.com/)

### Is VoiceInk free or paid?

**Both descriptions need context.** The source is GPLv3 and can be built yourself. The convenient prebuilt Mac application is sold as a **$25–$49 one-time license**, with a trial. [Source code](https://github.com/beingpax/VoiceInk) · [Prices](https://tryvoiceink.com/pricing)

### Is FluidVoice completely open source?

**Not every component.** The macOS app is GPLv3, but the maintainers say the Fluid Intelligence runtime is separately maintained and private. [Source](https://github.com/altic-dev/FluidVoice#fluid-intelligence)

### Does VoiceInk have real-time transcription and local AI cleanup?

**Yes.** Supported recognition models can show text while recording. VoiceInk Refine can clean up the result locally on a supported Apple Silicon Mac, but it cannot run custom context-aware prompts; use another compatible local provider for those. [Source](https://tryvoiceink.com/docs/voiceink-refine)

### Is Wispr Flow useless if I do not pay?

**No.** Its free plan includes dictation, dictionary features and limited meeting features, with weekly word allowances. If those limits fit your usage, you may have no reason to upgrade. [Source](https://wisprflow.ai/pricing)

## Final verdict

**My #1 is FluidVoice.** It offers the combination I care about most: **responsive live dictation, local AI-powered cleanup, a practical developer workflow, and no recurring app bill**.

**My #2 is VoiceInk.** It earns that place not because it is slower or less accurate—I have not proven either—but because I prioritize FluidVoice's free default experience over VoiceInk's richer customization. If your day depends on precise app/website rules, selected-text context and custom terminology, reverse that ranking.

**Wispr Flow still has its place** for broad cross-platform support, meeting functionality and teams. But I would test these local alternatives before committing to another $144 every year.
